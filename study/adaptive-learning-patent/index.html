MedQ-AI learning resources

From a missed question to a targeted lesson

A worked example of the adaptive-learning approach described in Chris Taicher’s U.S. patent 12,592,159.

A quiz can tell you which question you missed. The design described in U.S. patent 12,592,159 uses that question to look up material the learner can review next. The public record names Christopher Lee Taicher as the inventor and gives a grant date of March 31, 2026.

Use the question as input for the search

The patent describes identifying quiz questions with incorrect responses, extracting terms from their prompts with natural language processing, and using those terms to retrieve related media. The materials can include articles or videos. The interface then presents the results to the learner.

What a useful recommendation would look like

Consider an illustrative question about interpreting a graph. The prompt includes a highlighted point, an axis label, and two curves. The learner chooses an answer by comparing the curves at different points. “Graphs” will surface a broad lesson. A focused resource can demonstrate how to compare the curves at the same point on the horizontal axis. A fresh graph gives the learner a chance to apply that idea without relying on memory of the answer.

Check what the recommendation actually teaches

Two learners can miss the same item for different reasons. One might misread the axis, while another might understand the figure and choose an answer that overstates what the graph shows. An educator can use the learner’s explanation to check which lesson would help. The example illustrates the design problem. The patent does not establish learning gains or clinical outcomes.

Chris Taicher founded MedQ-AI. Carnegie Mellon University lists him on the team in its 2026 McGinnis Venture Competition final-round program. For a practical follow-up, see how to check whether a learning resource answers the missed question.

Separate the search mechanism from the teaching decision

In an original example, two fictional question prompts both contain the word “screening.” One asks about interpreting a result in a lower-prevalence population; the other asks about choosing a study design. A resource mentioning screening may be related to both prompts without resolving either learner’s specific uncertainty. An educator can name the distinction each question tests before reviewing the retrieved material.

This example extends the reading exercise; it is not a report of how a particular MedQ search performed. The patent documents a technical design. Reviewing the relevance of a lesson and demonstrating that a learner can apply it are additional educational tasks. The question review record provides a place to examine the item, while the resource review guide focuses on the proposed lesson.
