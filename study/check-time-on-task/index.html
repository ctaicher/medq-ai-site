Check time spent before crediting a study method

When two study groups receive different learning activities, compare the time and support built into each activity before attributing a score difference to one feature. The intervention may be a package of practice, feedback, reminders, and additional study time.

A fictional comparison

Imagine a randomized education study in which one group receives three 20-minute question sessions and the other receives one 20-minute reading session. At a later assessment, the groups’ mean scores are 78 and 70 out of 100. The observed difference is eight points.

The first group was assigned 60 minutes of activity; the second was assigned 20. The design compares those two packages. It does not isolate the effect of the question format while holding scheduled time constant. Additional practice, spacing, feedback, or other differences may contribute. These numbers are invented for teaching and are not MedQ-AI results.

Describe both packages

The TIDieR intervention-reporting checklist asks authors to describe session frequency, schedule, duration, and intensity or dose. It also distinguishes planned delivery from what was actually delivered. EQUATOR explains that a reporting checklist helps describe a study; it does not by itself establish design quality.

  • Assigned activity: What materials, practice, and feedback did each group receive?
  • Scheduled time: How many sessions were offered, how long was each, and how were they distributed?
  • Actual participation: How much activity did learners complete, and how was that measured?
  • Other support: Were reminders, coaching, or access to additional resources different?

Keep assignment and participation separate

Random assignment can support a comparison of the assigned packages when the study is otherwise conducted and analyzed appropriately. Learners who complete more sessions may also differ in motivation, availability, or starting knowledge. Comparing high-participation learners with everyone else does not automatically retain the protection of the original randomization.

Likewise, dividing each group’s mean score by scheduled minutes does not establish a learning-efficiency effect. A final score is not necessarily a gain from baseline, and scheduled minutes may not represent time actually spent. Treat an efficiency claim as a separate question requiring an appropriate outcome, time measure, and analysis.

Write a conclusion that fits

For the fictional example, a restrained note is: “The three-session question package had an eight-point higher mean assessment score than the single reading session. Assigned activity time differed, so this comparison does not isolate format alone.” A reader would still need uncertainty estimates, follow-up information, and the study’s other methods before judging the finding.
