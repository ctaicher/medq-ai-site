Separate randomization, concealment, and blinding

When a learning study calls itself randomized, read how the investigators formed the groups and measured the outcome. Three different safeguards can appear in the same study. Each addresses a different point in the process.

Follow a fictional study from enrollment to scoring

Imagine a study comparing two ways to teach an equipment check. Its report says: “A computer-generated random sequence assigned learners to either a video lesson or a coached session. An enrollment coordinator could see the full list. A separate assessor scored coded recordings without knowing the teaching group.” These details support different conclusions.

Three safeguards in the fictional report
QuestionWhat the report tells usWhat still needs checking
How were assignments generated?The report describes a computer-generated random sequence.Whether the method included chance. Alternating assignments would be predictable even if group sizes matched.
Who could know the next assignment before enrollment was confirmed?The enrollment coordinator could see the list.Whether knowledge of the next assignment could influence which learners entered or when. A random list alone does not provide concealment.
Who knew the group during outcome measurement?A separate assessor scored coded recordings.Whether the recordings or scoring materials revealed the group despite the coding.

Keep the timing of each safeguard clear

Allocation concealment protects the assignment process before and until a participant is assigned. Blinding concerns knowledge after assignment. A blinded assessor cannot repair a recruitment process influenced by advance knowledge of the next group.

In educational studies, learners and teachers may recognize the teaching format. That does not by itself show that assignment was unconcealed. Likewise, masking the assessor does not establish that participants or teachers were blinded. Name the people and the stage of the study instead of relying on an unexplained label such as “double blind.”

Write a precise reading note

For the fictional report, a useful note is: “Random sequence generation described; enrollment staff had access to future assignments; assessor masking described, but the recording content needs review.” This identifies the information and concern without declaring the entire study invalid or assuming that selective enrollment occurred.

Follow the report’s account of who enrolled participants, when eligibility and consent were confirmed, and when assignment was revealed. Then examine outcome measurement separately. Other issues, such as missing data or departures from the assigned teaching, need their own assessment.

The distinction between sequence generation, allocation concealment, and blinding follows Cochrane Handbook Chapter 8. The teaching example and reading note are original. They are not findings from a MedQ-AI trial or a complete risk-of-bias assessment.
