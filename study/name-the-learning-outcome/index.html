Name the outcome behind a learning claim

A learning tool can be evaluated through several kinds of evidence. A learner’s rating, a knowledge score, an observed action, and a patient outcome answer different questions. Before sharing a claim, identify which of those the study actually measured.

Read a fictional results summary

A hypothetical randomized study assigns 100 residents to Method A and 100 to Method B. Everyone completes a satisfaction survey and a knowledge test four weeks later. The study does not collect workplace observations or patient outcomes.

Illustrative results and the questions they address
MeasureFictional findingQuestion addressed
Satisfaction survey89 of 100 residents rate A positively; 85 of 100 rate B positively.How did respondents rate their experience?
Knowledge assessmentMean score 78/100 with A and 70/100 with B.How did the groups perform on this test at four weeks?
Workplace behaviorNot measured.How did residents apply learning in actual clinical work?
Patient outcomesNot measured.Did patient care outcomes differ?

The observed test-score difference is eight points. Its uncertainty and the planned analysis still need to be reviewed. The satisfaction numbers describe ratings. Neither row supplies the missing observations of clinical behavior or patient outcomes.

Match the wording to the measurement

“Residents assigned to A had a higher mean score on the four-week knowledge test” describes the table. “A improved patient care” would require evidence that the table does not contain. A positive experience may matter for selecting a tool, but it is a separate result from learning or clinical application.

Ragsdale and colleagues discuss these distinctions in their article on evaluating clinical education programs. They describe process and outcome measures and the Kirkpatrick categories of reaction, learning, behavior, and results. Use the categories to ask what information is available and what remains to be measured.

Plan the next question explicitly

If the aim is to assess application, define an observable clinical task, who will observe it, when it will be assessed, and what comparison is appropriate. If the aim is patient benefit, specify the patient outcome and a design that can address other influences on care. A knowledge-test result can motivate those questions without answering them in advance.

For a journal-club record, write the claim, the exact measurement, and one conclusion that the measurement does not establish. Then read the study’s assessment methods before deciding whether its claim fits.

Read Ragsdale et al., Evaluating the effectiveness of undergraduate clinical education programs. Continue with the guide to precision and comparison-group questions.
