Compare absolute and relative risk in the same study

A report that says an outcome fell by 50% leaves an important question for the reader. What were the risks in the two groups? Put both counts on the page before interpreting the comparison.

Use the same outcome and follow-up period

In a fictional randomized study, two groups each contain 1,000 participants followed for one year. The same defined outcome occurs in 20 people assigned to the comparison group and 10 assigned to the intervention group. Assume complete follow-up for this calculation.

Calculate the ratio and the difference

The risk ratio is 1% divided by 2%, or 0.5. The relative risk reduction is 1 minus 0.5, or 50%. The absolute risk reduction is 2% minus 1%, or 1 percentage point. That difference corresponds to 10 fewer people with the outcome per 1,000 participants over one year.

A study with risks of 0.2% and 0.1% would also produce a risk ratio of 0.5. Its absolute difference would be 0.1 percentage point, or one fewer person per 1,000. The baseline risk changes the absolute difference associated with the same relative reduction.

Keep the uncertainty and the study design in view

Find the confidence interval and check how participants were assigned, who was included in the analysis, and how outcomes were measured. Ask whether the follow-up period and population match the question you want to answer. These calculations describe the observed groups. Applying them to a new population takes an additional judgment about the evidence.

For the underlying definitions, see the CDC’s lessons on risk ratios and risk differences and proportional reductions. For a separate example involving educational outcomes, see reading study score differences.
