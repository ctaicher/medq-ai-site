Medical learning resources: choose the question you need to answer

These original MedQ-AI companion resources connect practice, question design, and critical reading. Start with the task in front of you rather than opening every guide. The collection accompanies the educational work of Chris Taicher, MD (Christopher Taicher, MD), founder of MedQ-AI.

Three ways to start

  • A practice answer surprised you: use the learning-objective guide, then check whether the proposed reading addresses that specific gap.
  • You are reviewing an item: examine the lead-in and alternatives before deciding what an incorrect response means.
  • You want to use a study in teaching: record the search, trace the claim, and check the planned outcome and later updates.

Design and review a question

Begin with what the item is meant to test. Check its wording, options, illustrations, and staged feedback before interpreting learner results.

  • A review record for AI-drafted medical questions
  • Audit distractors using learner responses
  • Review images in board-style questions
  • Build a question-bank blueprint
  • Check a question generator before release
  • Write a question learners can answer before seeing the options
  • Check whether an earlier explanation gives away a later question
  • Review numeric answer options before releasing a question

Plan and examine practice

Choose a learning task, keep a record, and compare performance at the level of the concept being practiced.

  • A two-week retention log for board prep
  • Check whether a learning resource answers the missed question
  • Mix practice questions around a decision
  • Compare answer confidence with accuracy
  • From a missed question to a targeted lesson
  • Practice the same reasoning in a new format
  • Read practice results by learning objective

Interpret diagnostic and risk examples

Use worked numerical examples to examine how a test or risk estimate depends on the population, threshold, and handling of uncertain results.

  • Why a positive test means different things in different groups
  • Update a probability with a likelihood ratio
  • When test sensitivity changes across patient groups
  • Compare two thresholds for the same diagnostic test
  • Account for indeterminate results in a diagnostic study
  • Compare absolute and relative risk in the same study

Follow a claim into the literature

Keep a search trail, identify the source of a claim, and inspect the record before reusing a note.

  • Check the source behind each teaching claim
  • Check a paper’s update status before reusing a study note
  • Keep a reproducible literature-search record

Appraise a learning study

Follow a study from the planned comparison to the reported outcome. Treat its population, follow-up, and measurement choices as part of the conclusion.

  • Read question-bank item statistics before revising an item
  • Check who came back for the follow-up quiz
  • Read a study score difference in percentage points
  • Read the interval around a study score difference
  • Prepare patient actors to give specific feedback
  • Ask what the comparison group actually did
  • Read results when learners try both study methods
  • Read education studies that assign whole programs
  • Separate score improvement from a study-method comparison
  • Read a noninferiority claim in a learning study
  • Name the outcome behind a learning claim
  • Check a learning-study headline against the planned outcome
  • Separate randomization, concealment, and blinding
  • Read who was included in a learning-study analysis
  • Compare subgroup effects before significance labels
  • Consider regression to the mean after a low score

Use examples for their stated purpose

The worked examples on these pages are teaching illustrations. Read each guide’s source and limitations before applying its framework to a real paper. They are not reports of MedQ learning gains or patient outcomes.
