Account for indeterminate results in a diagnostic study

An accuracy table may describe only the tests that produced a usable answer. Before applying its estimates, look for the people whose results were indeterminate and find out how the researchers handled them.

Follow all the people through the study

In this fictional study, a separate reference standard classifies 40 people as having the condition and 60 as not having it. The test under review gives a positive or negative answer for 80 people and an indeterminate result for 20.

Read the denominator beside each estimate

Among interpretable results, sensitivity is 27/(27 + 3) = 90% and specificity is 45/(45 + 5) = 90%. The test returned an interpretable result for 80/100 = 80% of everyone tested. Keeping these figures together shows the accuracy within the reported subset and how often the test gave a definite result.

In this example, indeterminate results occurred in 10/40 = 25% of people with the condition and 10/60, about 16.7%, of those without it. Ask what caused those results and whether the same problem could occur in the intended setting.

Find the next step for an indeterminate test

Read the methods for the planned response to an indeterminate result. Check whether a repeat sample, another test, or a clinical assessment was used, and whether the study reports the outcome of that step. If the accuracy table omits those participants, record both the omission and the reason given.

The AHRQ methods guide identifies removing uninterpretable results as a possible source of biased accuracy estimates. See its table of common biases and the related worked example of changing a test threshold.
