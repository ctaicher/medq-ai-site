Getting started with MedQ

Take a quiz. Use the results to choose a focused reading or video session. Then return to practice with a specific question in mind. This guide explains that learning loop for MedQ: Evolve with the Evidence.

Find the learning loop in version 2.1.1

The current release adds an animated introduction connecting quizzes with related PubMed summaries and videos. On Home, choose How MedQ works to replay it; use X to close it. An introduction on the first visit to Articles explains the evidence feed. Apple’s public release notes were checked November 27, 2026.

Use the introduction to orient yourself, then choose one learning task for the session. For example, after a question leaves you uncertain about a research population, write “Who was included?” before opening a related article. Afterward, record who was studied and one reason the findings may not apply to another setting. If the resource does not answer your question, keep that uncertainty visible rather than treating opening the article as completing the task.

The suggested note is an original companion exercise you can keep outside the app. The onboarding guide explains the workflow; it does not assess whether you understood the research. For version-specific changes, see the 2.1.1 release record.

Take a quiz in your specialty

Choose Emergency Medicine, Family Medicine, or General Surgery. Select a quiz length and use study mode when you want explanations as you work, or timed mode when you want a timed session. The app offers random or unattempted questions.

For an initial session, choose a length you can finish and review. Before looking at an explanation, briefly name the detail that drove your answer. This gives you something to compare with the explanation beyond whether the answer was correct.

Use the results to choose what to read or watch

The app identifies weaker categories and offers related articles and videos. Its public description calls this next learning direction Your Study Focus. Explore the resources related to the gap you want to examine.

Turn that gap into a question before opening the first resource. For example: “Which assumption did I make too quickly?” or “What population does this research actually describe?” After reading or watching, write one sentence answering your question. Keep the original source link when you want to return to a research finding.

Return to practice and explain the change

Try another question on the concept and explain the choice in your own words. If an answer changes as a case develops, identify the new information that changed the reasoning. Staged cases and variable-stem questions are included in the current release.

A correct answer on a repeated question can reflect familiarity with the item. To examine your reasoning more closely, ask what would happen in a different case or discuss the explanation with an educator. This suggested review routine is companion advice, not a claim of measured learning gains.

Common starting questions

Do I have to buy a pack to try the new specialties?
The U.S. listing describes 15 free questions in each of Family Medicine and General Surgery, followed by optional question packs. Emergency Medicine has a separate catalog. Check the app for current access and purchase details.
Does a recommended article settle a clinical question?
Read the source and its methods and limitations before relying on a summary. The source-checking guide can help you trace a statement back to its evidence.
What if the next resource does not address my question?
Keep the unanswered question explicit. Use the resource-review guide to distinguish a general overview from an answer to that particular learning need.
Where can I report a problem?
Contact support@medq-ai.com with the app version, specialty, screen, and the issue you observed. Use question identifiers when available and exclude patient information.
