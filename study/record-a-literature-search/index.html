Keep a literature-search record you can explain later

A useful study note records more than the paper you eventually read. It also records the question that led you there and how you searched. This companion to Chris Taicher’s MedQ-AI learning resources helps learners return to a search without guessing what they did last time.

Preserve what PubMed actually searched

The National Library of Medicine’s PubMed User Guide explains that Search Details, available in Advanced Search history, show the strategy PubMed used, including translations and warnings. Save both your entered query and those details. Selected filters persist across searches until cleared or browser data is cleared, so record which ones were active.

History is temporary: NLM states that it expires after eight hours without activity. A URL based only on history numbers such as #1 and #2 will not preserve the underlying searches after that history expires. Copy the full query into your own record rather than relying on those numbers.

A worked question about medical education

Suppose your study group wants to investigate whether a spaced question schedule affects later knowledge assessment. This is a search-planning example, not a report of a completed review or a claim about MedQ’s effectiveness.

  1. Write the question first. “Among medical learners, how do repeated question sessions compare with a single session for a later knowledge test?” Record any setting or learner group that matters to your purpose.
  2. Choose an exploratory query. A starting example is (spaced repetition OR distributed practice) AND medical education. It is not a validated comprehensive strategy. Record its actual translation after running it rather than assuming each phrase behaves as expected.
  3. Make one deliberate revision. If the results are too broad, decide which concept needs clarification. Preserve the earlier query and write what you changed. Do not silently add several restrictions and later describe the final result set as the original search.
  4. Record why a paper is worth reading. For example: “Includes the learner group of interest and reports a later assessment.” After opening it, check whether the methods actually match that reason.

A reusable search log

Purpose
The question and intended use: personal study, journal club, or preparation for a broader review.
Search record
Database, date searched, exact entered query, translated strategy, warnings, and active filters.
Scope choices
Any date, language, population, or article-type restriction, with a short reason for each.
Retrieval
Result count at that time, sort order, and the identifiers of papers saved for reading. Leave fields blank until actually checked.
Revision trail
Previous query, new query, and the reason for the change.
Reading decision
Why a paper was selected and whether the full text supports that decision. Distinguish “not relevant” from “not accessible.”

Keep the claim within the search

A quick study search can identify useful reading without establishing that every relevant study has been found. Describe its scope honestly. If the task requires a systematic review, get appropriate search-methods support and use a documented review process.

When you revisit the search, retain the earlier log and add a new dated entry. A changed result count is a reason to inspect new or revised records; it is not, by itself, evidence that a clinical conclusion changed.
