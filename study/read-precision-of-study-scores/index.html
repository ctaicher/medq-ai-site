Read the interval around a study score difference

Two studies can report the same average difference and give you different amounts of information about its size. The confidence interval describes the statistical precision of the estimate under the method and assumptions used. Read it alongside the estimate, the comparison, and the outcome.

Compare two fictional trials

Both trials report that residents assigned to a new practice schedule scored an average of six percentage points higher on a four-week assessment than residents using the usual schedule. Positive values favor the new schedule. The numbers below are invented for this example.

Mean difference+6 points+6 points
95% confidence interval+5 to +7 points−4 to +16 points

Ask what difference would matter

Suppose faculty decided before the study that a four-point average improvement would justify the staff time. Trial A’s interval lies above that threshold. Trial B’s interval includes differences large enough to justify the time, changes below the threshold, and a possible decline. The same six-point estimate leaves room for very different decisions.

Check what the interval leaves unresolved

Before acting on either result, check how the study selected learners, measured quiz scores, and handled missing data. An interval measures precision under the statistical assumptions. Study design problems can affect the estimate even when the interval is narrow.

The CONSORT 2025 statement asks authors to report estimated effects and their precision. See the score difference example for interpreting units, and the follow-up quiz example for checking who contributed results.
