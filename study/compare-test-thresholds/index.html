Compare two thresholds for the same diagnostic test

A diagnostic test that reports a number needs a rule for calling the result positive. When you read an accuracy study, find that rule before comparing its sensitivity or specificity with another report.

Keep the patients and the reference standard fixed

In this fictional example, 100 people receive the same numeric test. A separate reference standard classifies 40 people as having the condition and 60 as not having it. Higher test values suggest the condition. The researchers compare two definitions of a positive result using the same measurements.

Calculate each measure with its own denominator

At the lower cutoff, sensitivity is 36/40 = 90% and specificity is 42/60 = 70%. At the higher cutoff, sensitivity is 28/40 = 70% and specificity is 54/60 = 90%. Raising the cutoff changes some previously positive results to negative. In this example, that includes 8 people with the condition and 12 people without it.

The higher cutoff produces fewer false positives and more false negatives. Ask which consequence matters for the proposed use and what happens after a positive or negative result. The counts alone do not establish a clinical cutoff for a real test.

Check how the study chose its cutoff

Look for a threshold chosen before the results were analyzed. If investigators tried many cutoffs and reported the best-performing one in that same dataset, ask whether its performance was checked in another group. AHRQ’s methods guide identifies this selection process as a possible source of overly optimistic accuracy estimates.
