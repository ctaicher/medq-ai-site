Practice the same reasoning in a new format

A correct multiple-choice answer tells you what the learner selected. Asking the learner to explain the same reasoning in a simulated conversation reveals what they can express when the answer choices are gone. Larsen and colleagues studied repeated retrieval with written tests and standardized patients and reported benefits for long-term retention and for applying knowledge in simulation.

Start with a written problem

A fictional test detects 90 of every 100 people with a condition in a study. The prompt asks what that finding tells you. A complete answer identifies the group in the denominator: 100 people who have the condition. The result describes the proportion detected among those people.

Change the context and the task

Later, give the learner a new figure: a different fictional test detected 85 of 100 people with a condition in a study. A simulated patient asks, “My result was negative. Is there a 15% chance I have it?” Ask the learner to answer in ordinary language, then explain what information they would need to estimate the chance after a negative result.

A response might be: “The study tells us how often the test missed the condition among people who already had it. To interpret a negative result for you, we would need to know how likely the condition was before the test and how often the test is negative in people without it.”

Score the reasoning and the explanation

  • Reasoning: Did the learner distinguish people with the condition from people who tested negative?
  • Missing information: Did they identify the starting probability and how the test performs among people without the condition?
  • Explanation: Did they use clear language and avoid giving the simulated patient a probability the data cannot support?

If you only want to check the reasoning, accept an accurate plain-language explanation with any wording. If communication is part of the goal, score it separately.
