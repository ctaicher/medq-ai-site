Compare subgroup effects before comparing significance labels

A learning intervention can have an uncertain effect in one subgroup and a clearer estimate in another. That pattern alone does not establish that the intervention works differently for those groups. First compare the effect estimates and ask whether their difference was assessed directly.

Read the size of the difference

Imagine a fictional education study comparing Method A with Method B on the same 100-point assessment. Learners are classified by a baseline experience measure into two nonoverlapping subgroups. The report gives these hypothetical between-method differences, calculated as A minus B:

Fictional effects within two learner subgroups
SubgroupEstimated difference95% confidence interval
Less prior experience+6 points+2 to +10
More prior experience+6 points−2 to +14

Both point estimates are +6. The second interval is wider and includes zero. Calling the first result “significant” and the second “not significant” would not turn identical point estimates into evidence of a difference between subgroups. The intervals describe uncertainty around each within-subgroup comparison; they are not a direct interval for the difference between those effects.

Find the comparison that answers the claim

If a headline says Method A benefits less-experienced learners more, look for an appropriate direct comparison of the effects, often expressed as an interaction test. Read its estimate and uncertainty where reported. Neither two different significance labels nor a visual judgment about overlapping intervals replaces that comparison.

An inconclusive interaction test also does not prove identical effects. Check whether the data can meaningfully distinguish differences relevant to the educational question. This example does not provide the underlying data needed for a full analysis.

Check how the subgroup question was chosen

  • Was the subgroup definition established before outcomes were examined?
  • Was it based on a baseline characteristic or on something that happened during the intervention?
  • How many subgroup comparisons were explored?
  • Does the reported conclusion reflect the direct comparison and its uncertainty?

For the fictional table, a precise reading note is: “The estimated benefit was six points in each experience subgroup, with different precision. The table alone does not support a claim of greater benefit in one subgroup.” All numbers and the teaching example are original and hypothetical, not results from MedQ-AI.

The interpretation principle follows Cochrane Handbook Chapter 10, section 10.11.3.1. Continue with reading the precision of study scores and checking the planned outcome.
